Use timber framing where exposed posts and beams suit the building, such as halls, houses or upper storeys.
- Lay out structural-looking bays around doors, windows and floor heights. Put posts at corners and openings; repeat intermediate posts at a consistent spacing that fits the wall, rather than forcing a four-block grid.
- Infill can be calcite, diorite, pale plaster-like blocks or planks. Choose a contrasting wood for the frame and a related stone for the base. A castle's masonry keep does not require this treatment.
- Vertical posts use logs with axis y. Beams follow the wall with axis x or z at floor lines and under eaves. Flush framing is restrained; a one-block projection creates stronger depth when space allows.
- Build infill first, then posts and beams. Check that the final frame does not close a window or narrow a doorway.
- A projecting upper storey can rest on visible brackets or corbels. Trapdoors, fences and gates suggest brackets at small scale; keep them out of paths and headroom.
- Ground floors may be stone, wood or a mix appropriate to the request. Avoid making every medieval building the same stone-box-and-timber-top recipe.
- Let a few beams, shutters or lanterns explain the construction. Extra trim should support the bay rhythm rather than compete with it.
