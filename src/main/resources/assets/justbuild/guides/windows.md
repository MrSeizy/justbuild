Place openings to serve rooms and reinforce the facade's structure.
- Start with the wall's bays, floor heights and interior layout. Align related windows and vary them by use: narrow defensive openings, larger residential windows or broad modern glazing.
- Panes can sit in the wall plane; recess them when wall thickness allows. A one-block-thick wall cannot support a deep recess without changing the interior.
- Scale the frame to the opening. Posts, slabs or upside-down stairs can form jambs and lintels; thick stone walls may use a deeper arch. Frames are optional on a deliberately plain facade.
- Small traditional windows may be one wide and two tall. Treat this as one option, not the size for every building. Leave enough solid wall between large openings.
- Shutters, sills and flower boxes belong only where the style and available space support them. A flower box needs a visible planting surface and should not obstruct the route below.
- Bay windows project usable space beyond the wall and need a base and roof connection. Attic windows belong in gable ends or dormers, not pasted flat onto a roof slope.
- Keep the entrance distinguishable from nearby windows through position, scale or framing. Check openings after later beams, trim and roof operations.
